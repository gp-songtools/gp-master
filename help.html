1. What the app does, in order

Everything runs in your browser on your own machine. No audio is uploaded, and there is no account, server or API key involved.

In Full master mode the signal passes through these stages, in this order:

1 Analysis — measures the song's tone across eight octave bands, its stereo width, and how much low-mid energy sits in the sides
2 Static EQ — a 25 Hz high-pass plus up to eight corrective filters aiming at the target curve
3 Dynamic EQ — four bands that cut only when they jump above their own running average
4 Vocal enhancer — centre-channel leveling, presence and air (off unless you turn it on)
5 Clarity — a high-pass on the side signal, and a small centre cut at 140 Hz
6 Band split — below 150 Hz, 150 Hz to 2.5 kHz, above 2.5 kHz
7 Multiband compression — each band compressed against its own average level
8 Pumper — the kick ducks the mid and high bands (off unless you turn it on)
9 Stereo stage — bass summed to mono, mid and high width adjusted
10 De-esser — reduces 5–9 kHz when it spikes
11 Saturation — peak soft-clip, plus optional Tape, Tube or Crunch harmonics
12 Tone EQ — your own low, mid and high sliders (flat unless you move them)
13 Loudness match and limiter — iterates gain until the target is hit, then true-peak limits
14 Dither — triangular, applied on conversion to 24-bit or 16-bit

If Stem balance is active, separation and the per-stem chains happen before stage 1, and what reaches stage 1 is your rebalanced mix rather than the original file.

2. A workflow that avoids the usual mistakes

  1. Load reference tracks first. Without them the app aims at a hand-chosen curve that may not suit your material. With them it aims at something measured. This is the single biggest quality difference available to you.
  2. Use Preview 30 s, not Master full song. It renders a 30-second section from the middle of the track in a fraction of the time, and the A/B player and spectrum view work identically on it.
  3. Start on Gentle. Move up only if the result sounds under-processed to you.
  4. A/B with loudness matching on. Louder reliably sounds better to human ears, which is exactly why a fair comparison needs the levels matched.
  5. Press "Listen in mono" before you commit. This is where stereo processing goes wrong, and it will not be obvious on headphones.
  6. Then render the full song and check the Mono fold figure in the results.

Automatic mastering improves some material and makes other material worse. There is no setting that removes the need to listen. Treat every number this app reports as a measurement, and every suggestion as an opinion.

3. Reference tracks

Load up to five songs whose balance you like. The app measures each one and aims for the average of those measurements instead of its built-in target.

What it measures, and what it ignores

Measured and copiedNot measured
Octave-band tone curve, level-normalizedArrangement and instrumentation
Overall stereo width (side-to-mid ratio)Loudness or dynamic range
Low-mid side energy, 160–450 HzAnything time-varying

Only those measurements are kept. The reference audio is analyzed and immediately discarded, which is why stored references take about 300 bytes each and why nothing you load is retained or transmitted.

Choosing references

  • Match the material, not your taste. The app copies tone and width, so a reference needs comparable instrumentation and arrangement density. A sparse acoustic track and a dense guitar track have genuinely different spectra; averaging them produces a target that matches neither.
  • Use real files. Lossless downloads or your own CD rips. Streaming services are loudness-normalized and will not give you a file; YouTube rips are lossy re-encodes that have often been altered again on upload.
  • Do not pay for hi-res. The app works at 44.1 kHz and measures average tone, so a 16-bit/44.1 kHz FLAC is exactly as useful as a 24-bit/192 kHz file.
  • Beware of versions. Remasters, vinyl masters and streaming masters of the same song differ. Vinyl rips in particular carry cutting and playback EQ you do not want to copy.
  • Two or three, not one. A single master may be an unusual outlier.

The agreement check

Averaging references that disagree produces a target matching none of them, so the app measures how far each reference sits from their average and reports it. Both curves are level-normalized first, so this is purely about tone shape.

ReadingMeans
Under 1.5 dBThey agree. Normal variation between well-mastered songs sits near 0.9 dB.
Over 1.5 dBThey disagree. The app names the reference furthest from the rest and the frequency range where they diverge most. Remove the named one.

With three or more references the outlier is named explicitly and marked in the list. With two, both sit equally far from their average, so you compare the faint lines on the spectrum view instead.

The 1.5 dB line is a judgment call, but it was calibrated against real files rather than guessed: two different but sanely mastered songs measured 0.9 dB apart, while a reference with a deliberate 9 dB tilt error measured 2.4–3.2 dB.

What turning references on actually changes

  • The tone target becomes the measured reference curve instead of the genre curve.
  • Stereo width on Auto aims at the references' measured width instead of a guess, clamped to between 0.7× and 1.4×.
  • Clarity on Auto triggers only when your song has more low-mid side energy than the references do, rather than against a fixed threshold.

Unchecking "Match tone and width" reverts all three to the genre behaviour without unloading anything.

4. Saved setups

A setup is a name attached to two things: your loaded reference measurements, and every setting on the page. Saving under an existing name replaces it.

Separately, your current settings are saved automatically as you change them, so the app reopens where you left it even if you never save a named setup.

Export and import

Export writes all named setups plus your current references and settings to gp-master-setups.json. Import merges a file back, appending "(2)" on a name collision rather than overwriting, so importing someone else's file cannot destroy your own setups. Corrupt reference entries inside an imported file are dropped and reported rather than silently producing a wrong target.

Browser storage is per-browser and per-device. It never syncs between machines, and it is erased when you clear site data. Export is the only durable copy — use it before clearing anything, and to move setups between computers.

6. Target loudness

Integrated loudness in LUFS, measured per ITU-R BS.1770 with K-weighting and gating — the same standard streaming services use. The app iterates gain and limiting until the measured output lands within 0.1 LU of your target.

TargetUse
−16podcasts, spoken word
−14streaming reference point; preserves the most dynamics
−11 to −9competitively loud; what most modern releases measure
−8 and abovevery loud; expect audible limiting on most material

Streaming services turn loud masters down rather than rewarding them, so a −9 LUFS master played on Spotify is quieter and more squashed than a −14 LUFS one. Loud masters are worth making for download, local playback, and because quiet masters sound weak when a listener compares them manually.

If the target is unreachable without extreme limiting the app stops at the closest achievable value and says so in the results.

Tone EQ

Three sliders under the loudness target: Low is a shelf at 100 Hz, Mid a broad bell at 1 kHz (Q 0.7), High a shelf at 8 kHz, each ±6 dB in 0.5 dB steps. Double-click a slider to zero it; Reset zeroes all three.

You hear it while you move it. It runs live on the waveform player, on Play all stems (not on the single-stem ▶ buttons), and on the B: Master side of the results player. A: Original is never EQ'd, so A/B still compares against the untouched song. Bypass switches it off while you listen; renders always use the sliders.

In a render it sits after the whole mastering chain and before loudness matching and the limiter, so the result still hits your target and ceiling. A low boost therefore does not make the song louder; it gives the limiter more to do. Watch the true peak and listen for pumping if you push Low.

Once a result exists, moving a slider plays the change live on the master and shows Apply EQ to the master. That re-runs only the last stage (EQ, loudness match, limiter, encoding), which takes seconds rather than a full re-master, and keeps your place in the player. Save file is disabled until you apply, so the file you save is always the one you heard. What you hear live before applying is very close but not exact, because the limiter has not re-run yet.

7. Output format

FormatDetailCeiling
WAV24-bit PCM, triangular dither−1.0 dBTP
MP3320 kbps−1.5 dBTP
M4AAAC 256 kbps−1.5 dBTP

The lossy formats get 0.5 dB more headroom because encoders overshoot: the decoded file can peak higher than the file you fed in. Use WAV for anything you will distribute or process further, and the lossy formats for sharing and listening.

Peak levels are measured as true peak with 4× sinc oversampling, so the number reported accounts for inter-sample peaks that a simple sample-peak meter misses.

8. Volume automation

Mastering normally applies one setting to the whole song, but problems are usually local: one chorus that jumps forward, a verse sitting too low, an intro you want left alone. This is where you ride the fader instead.

Zoom and exact times. Under the waveform, + and − zoom in and out (Ctrl or Cmd with the mouse wheel zooms at the pointer), Fit shows the whole song and Zoom to region frames the selected region. A slider moves along the zoomed song, and the view follows the playhead while it plays. Each region's start and end are editable in minutes and seconds, such as 1:23.5, or plain seconds. Add by time creates a region from a typed start and end, and Now copies the playhead position.

Drag across the waveform to select a part of the song. Each region gets one of three modes:

ModeWhat it does
LevelChanges the level by up to ±12 dB before the mastering chain, so the compressors respond to the corrected balance instead of fighting it. This is what fader riding means.
Tame dynamicsAdds compression inside the region only — Light, Medium or Strong, up to 6 dB of reduction measured against the region’s own running level. Use it on a section that is dynamically wilder than the rest.
No tone processingKeeps the original tone there — no EQ, no compression, no saturation — but still applies the final loudness match and limiting, so the level matches the rest of the song and there is no jump at the boundary.

Things worth knowing

  • Play the song first. Press Play under the waveform to listen and find the part you want. Clicking the waveform moves the playhead, and clicking inside a region also selects it. The source player and the A/B master player never sound at the same time.
  • Edges are faded. Each boundary gets a 20 ms raised-cosine ramp, so transitions do not click.
  • Preview follows your selection. With a region selected, Preview renders around that region plus a few seconds of context instead of the middle of the song, so you hear what you just set.
  • Loudness is still held at your target, which has a consequence worth understanding. Turning one section down makes the whole track quieter, so the loudness match raises everything to compensate. Measured on real material, a −9 dB region ended up 7.3 dB down relative to its surroundings while the rest of the song came up about 1 dB. The relative balance is what you set; absolute levels shift to hold the target.
  • Regions belong to the loaded file and are cleared when you load another. They are not stored in setups, because they describe one specific song rather than a way of working.
  • Click a region on the waveform, or its time range in the list, to select it. Colour indicates the mode.

Regions are also the natural way to point at part of a song for other purposes. Selecting a quiet passage is how a future noise-reduction stage would learn the noise profile.

9. Noise reduction

Removes steady background noise: hiss, hum, room tone, and the warble AI generators sometimes leave behind. It measures the noise floor in each frequency band, then attenuates each band as it drops toward that floor.

It runs first, before any EQ or compression, so the rest of the chain never amplifies noise.

SettingMaximum reduction
Light6 dB
Medium12 dB
Strong20 dB

Where the noise profile comes from

Mark a quiet passage — an intro, a gap, a tail — with a Noise profile region on the waveform. That measures the actual noise instead of inferring it. A noise region is a measurement marker only: it does not change how that part is processed.

Without one, the floor is estimated from the quietest 5% of each frequency band across the song.

Measured on a file with known added noise

White noise at −48 dBFS was added to a clean excerpt, with three seconds of noise alone at the start, then Medium (12 dB) was applied:

Profile sourceNoise removedMusic lost
Marked region12.0 dB0.45 dB
Automatic (5th percentile)11.5 dB0.43 dB
Automatic (15th percentile)11.9 dB3.34 dB

The third row is why the automatic estimate uses the 5th percentile: the 15th removed only 0.4 dB more noise while costing nearly 3 dB of music. A marked region remains the more reliable choice, because it measures the noise rather than inferring it from statistics, but the gap is small on material like this.

Limits

  • Steady noise only. It models a constant noise floor, so it will not remove a cough, a click, or a passing car.
  • Strong settings can sound watery. Gains are smoothed across frequency and over time to avoid the warbling that makes denoisers obvious, but on dense music 20 dB is audible. Check on Preview.
  • It cannot separate noise from quiet music. Reverb tails and soft passages sit near the noise floor and will be attenuated with it.

10. Transients

Two envelope followers run on the signal, one fast and one slow. Where the fast one runs ahead of the slow one, an attack is happening. The drive is that gap’s excess over its own one-second running average, which self-calibrates per track rather than using a fixed threshold — measured on real material, the gap sits near 2.1 dB in steady passages and 3.5 dB at onsets, so any fixed threshold would be wrong for the next track.

SettingEffect on attacks
Softenup to −3 dB
Punchyup to +3 dB
Very punchyup to +6 dB

It runs after saturation and before the limiter. That ordering matters: placed before saturation, the soft-clipping stage squashed exactly the peaks the shaper had just raised, which inverted the control entirely.

What it actually does, measured

On a test signal with genuine transients — percussive hits over a sustained pad — Very punchy applies about 5.5 dB of gain at onsets against 1.6 dB during steady passages, and raises short-term crest factor by about 0.11 dB. A gain release of 30 ms measured best; shorter releases performed slightly worse.

Be realistic about the size of this effect. What it mainly does is bring percussive hits forward relative to sustained sounds. It does not make individual hits noticeably sharper, because the gain release is longer than a transient, so each hit is lifted as a whole. Through the full chain at a fixed loudness target the net change in crest factor can be near zero, since the surrounding compression and limiting re-level what the shaper boosts.

It has the most to work with on an unlimited mix at a moderate loudness target. On a source that is already a loud master, expect very little.

11. Stem balance

This splits your song into vocals, drums, bass and other, lets you rebalance them, optionally masters each one separately, then sums them and masters the result.

Separating

Separation uses neural network models that download once — about 123 MB total — and are then cached by your browser. It is slow, but it runs in parallel: the song is cut into independent chunks and shared between several background workers, up to four, chosen from your processor's core count and leaving one core free for the page. In testing, two workers finished the same job in about 53% of the time one worker needed, with output identical sample for sample. Measured on a 2-core test machine, a 3.5-minute song still takes tens of minutes on one worker, so a desktop with more cores helps a lot. Use Separate 30 s section first; it covers the same section the preview uses.

Vocals, drums and bass each come from their own model. "Other" is derived by subtracting those three from your mix, so the four stems always sum back to the original exactly.

While it runs, the status line shows how many chunks are done, how many workers are in use, and a countdown of the total time remaining for the whole job. The bar is weighted by how much work each stem is. The estimate starts once the first chunk finishes: it takes the median compute time of each stem's finished chunks, predicts stems that have not started from a measured one using the cost ratio of roughly 3.2 : 1 : 1 for vocals, drums and bass (timed in Chromium on a 2-core machine), divides by the number of workers, and adds a model load for each stem not yet started. In simulations it ran about 10 to 25 percent high early on and tightened as stems completed. Model downloads on first use are not included. Treat it as "about".

Memory: each worker needs roughly 1 to 1.2 GB while it runs (measured with the real models: about 1.2 GB per vocal worker and 1 GB per drums worker), mostly the network’s working memory rather than the model file. Four workers is about 4 to 5 GB, and eight is about 8 to 10 GB, so pick a worker count your computer’s memory can hold. If a worker fails, its chunk goes back in the queue for the others, and the job fails only if none can finish it.

Use a desktop browser. Separation needs considerably more memory than most phones have, and a full-length song may fail outright on mobile.

Rebalancing

Each stem gets a level slider from −24 to +12 dB and M (mute) and S (solo) buttons like a mixing desk. Mute leaves that stem out of the mastered result; solo is for listening only (in Play all stems, soloed stems play and the rest go quiet) and does not affect the master. Changes are instant once separation is done — it does not re-separate.

Workers. Auto uses up to four workers, based on your core count with one core left for the page. The Workers menu lets you choose 1 to 8 (never more than your core count) and remembers the choice on this device. More workers help only while there are free cores and free memory; measured on one desktop, four workers gave a 2.9× speedup over one, so beyond that you are trading memory for diminishing returns. The estimate beside the menu is about 1.1 GB per worker.

Choosing and saving stems. Tick which of vocals, drums and bass to separate; anything you skip stays in Other, and fewer stems finish sooner. Each separated stem shows its own waveform, normalized to that stem's peak with the peak level printed in the corner, so even a quiet stem is readable. Click a waveform to jump there. The ⬇ WAV button beside it saves that stem as a 24-bit stereo WAV named after your song, with _section added if you separated only the 30-second section. The separate Stem Splitter page has been folded into this section.

Listening to the stems. Each stem row has a round ▶ button that plays that stem alone, leveled up toward a common loudness (capped at +18 dB) so a quiet stem such as bass is audible. That is for judging how clean the separation is, not how loud the stem is in the mix. Play all stems mixes them at the levels and mute/solo settings you have set, and follows the sliders live while it plays, so you can hear a rebalance before you master it. This is the stems summed, not the mastered result. A shared position bar lets you scrub, and switching between buttons keeps your place, which makes it easy to compare a stem against the full mix at the same moment. Only the separated section plays, and starting the waveform player or the A/B player stops it.

When separation finishes the app measures each stem against the full mix and suggests moves, with one button to apply them all. The targets per genre:

PresetVocalsDrumsBassOther
Rock−9 to −5−7 to −3−13 to −8−8 to −3
Pop−8 to −4−8 to −4−12 to −7−10 to −5
Hip-hop−8 to −4−6 to −2−9 to −4−12 to −6
Electronic−10 to −5−6 to −2−9 to −4−8 to −3
Acoustic−7 to −3−9 to −5−14 to −9−8 to −3
Voice−5 to −1−14 to −8−18 to −11−14 to −8

These ranges are rules of thumb I chose, not measurements taken from reference recordings, and they take no account of your arrangement. A deliberately buried vocal will be flagged as wrong when it is not. Suggested moves are capped at 6 dB.

Mastering each stem separately

With this on, each stem gets its own chain before the stems are summed:

StemEQDynamics
Vocals90 Hz high-pass, +1.5 dB at 3 kHz, +1 dB above 11 kHz2.5:1, max 4 dB, de-esser up to 5 dB
Drums30 Hz high-pass, +1 dB at 65 Hz, +1 dB above 9 kHz2:1, fast 4 ms attack, max 4 dB
Bass28 Hz high-pass, +0.8 dB at 90 Hz, mono below 150 Hz3:1, slow 20 ms attack, max 5 dB
Other40 Hz high-pass, −1 dB at 320 Hz1.8:1, max 3 dB

Each compressor works against that stem's own running average rather than a fixed threshold, so it adapts to the material.

The limiter deliberately stays on the summed mix rather than running per stem. Limiting each stem to full loudness and then summing them would clip, and would undo the balance you just set.

Vocal doubler

An optional thickener for the vocal stem. It keeps only 180 Hz to 9 kHz of the vocal and adds two copies: one about 19 ms late leaning left, one about 27 ms late leaning right. Each copy's delay drifts slowly, which detunes it by up to about 10 cents, so it behaves like a second and third take instead of a fixed echo. Light, Medium and Thick set the copies at −14, −10 and −7 dB below the lead.

It only works on a separated vocal, so it does nothing until you split the song, and the results panel says "skipped" if it was on without stems. Any bleed or artifacts in the vocal stem are doubled with it, so start at Light and listen. Delayed copies can comb-filter when the stereo image folds to mono: check Listen in mono and the mono-compatibility row.

Scope

Stems only apply to the section you separated. If you separate 30 seconds and then render the full song, the app masters the original audio instead and tells you so — separate the whole song to apply your balance to the whole song.

Separated stems carry artifacts: bleed between stems, watery high frequencies, and smearing on cymbals and reverb tails. This is a rescue tool for a mix you cannot re-open. If you have the original session, exporting real stems from it will always be better.

12. Processing mode

ModeWhat runs
Full masterEverything in the chain above. Analyzes the song and adapts.
Light polishFixed EQ — 28 Hz high-pass, −1.5 dB at 60 Hz, +0.8 dB at 3.5 kHz, +2 dB above 10 kHz — plus one gentle compressor. Identical for every song.
Loudness onlyNo tone changes at all. Just loudness matching and limiting.

Loudness only is the honest choice when you trust your mix and want nothing but a level change. It is also the right mode for checking what the other modes are actually doing to you: master once in Loudness only, once in Full master, and compare.

Clarity, stereo width, the vocal enhancer, the pumper and the processing amount all apply to Full master only.

13. Processing amount

This scales every automatic decision at once. On Gentle it also raises the dynamic EQ and de-esser thresholds by 3 dB so they react only to clear problems rather than working continuously.

AmountTone EQDynamic EQ / de-essCompressionSaturation
Gentle55%, capped ±1.4 dB55% / 50%, +3 dB threshold55%40%
Standard100%, capped ±2.5 dB100%100%100%
Strong140%, capped ±3.5 dB140% / 130%, −2 dB threshold140%130%

Measured on real material, Gentle made four tone moves up to 1.4 dB with the dynamic EQ averaging 0.0 dB and peaking at 1.6 dB; Strong made seven moves up to 3.5 dB with the dynamic EQ averaging 0.8 dB and peaking at 4.2 dB.

Gentle is the default because over-processing is harder to hear than under-processing, and much harder to undo. Start there.

Saturation setting and character

The Saturation control sits separately from the amount. Auto is the gentle peak soft-clip in the table above. Off bypasses it. Light, Medium and Heavy add real harmonic saturation on top, in one of three characters:

CharacterShapeSounds likeRange
TapeSymmetric, softSmooth density, mostly odd harmonics, highs rolled off above 12 kHzAbove 120 Hz
TubeLopsidedWarmth from even harmonics, including an octave aboveAbove 120 Hz
CrunchBlunt, reacts to quiet material tooGrit and biteAbove 250 Hz

The three levels are calibrated to add about −36, −28 and −21 dB of nonlinear content relative to the signal, measured on a real 40-second track and reproduced within 1 dB in the full chain. Only the harmonics and peak rounding are added back, so the fundamentals keep their level, and the results panel reports the figure for your song. Aliasing is controlled with anti-aliased waveshaping, which measured 15 to 19 dB lower aliasing than a plain shaper on a 10 kHz test tone.

The loudness stage still lands on your target, so heavier saturation changes density and tone, not volume. It applies to Full master only. Heavy Crunch is a deliberate effect; for a clean master, stay on Auto or Light.

Distortion

Distortion is deliberate grit, separate from Saturation. It builds a distorted copy of the signal (kept above 100–200 Hz so the bass stays clean), level-matches that copy to its dry counterpart by measuring your song, and blends it underneath at −24, −17 or −11 dB for Light, Medium and Heavy. Measured on a real track the finished result adds about −26, −19 and −13 dB relative to the signal, and the three types agree within 1.5 dB, so the levels mean the same whichever you choose.

TypeWhat it doesSounds like
OverdriveSoft, lopsided clip at 3, 6 or 12× driveGuitar-pedal warmth, fairly musical
FuzzHard clip at 12, 30 or 80× driveBuzzy, aggressive, every harmonic at once
Crush10, 8 or 6 bits, held for 2, 3 or 4 samplesDigital, lo-fi, aliasing on purpose

Overdrive and Fuzz use anti-aliased waveshaping, which measured 10 to 14 dB lower aliasing than a plain shaper on a 10 kHz tone. Fuzz is still raw by design. Crush aliases deliberately.

On a whole mix this adds noise-like content in the mids, so keep it to Light there. It is usually better on single stems: with stems separated, the stem panel has a Stem distortion selector for each of vocals, drums, bass and other, using the type chosen under Distortion. The stem is distorted after its own EQ and compression and before its level fader, and the results panel lists what each stem received.

14. Clarity

This addresses one specific problem: wide low-mid content making a mix sound muddy. When reverb, wide guitars or pads spread energy between roughly 150 and 500 Hz across the stereo field, the result is congestion that a plain EQ cut cannot fix without thinning the whole track.

Clarity high-passes the side signal only, pulling wide low-mid content toward the centre while leaving the centre's weight alone. At stronger settings it also applies a small cut at 140 Hz on the centre.

SettingSide high-passCentre cut
Off——
Light160 Hz—
Medium200 Hz—
Strong320 Hz−1.5 dB at 140 Hz
Automeasures your low-mid side energy and chooses, scaled by the processing amount

With references loaded, Auto compares your song to them instead of to a fixed threshold, and does nothing if your song is already tighter than the references.

Clarity improves mono compatibility, since it removes side content that would cancel on mono playback. If the mono fold figure concerns you, this is a safe control to push, unlike stereo width.

If a mix is muddy because several instruments occupy the same frequencies in the centre, Clarity will not help. That is a mix problem; fix it in the arrangement or with stem balance.

15. Stereo width

Scales the side signal of the mid and high bands. Bass below 150 Hz is summed to mono at every setting, which keeps the low end solid and is standard mastering practice.

SettingSide levelMono risk
Narrow0.7×none; improves mono
Normal1.0×, unchangednone
Wide1.2×some
Extra wide1.4×real
Autofrom the references' measured width if loaded, otherwise 1.0–1.25× from your song's own width

This is the control most likely to ruin your master on a phone. The side signal is L−R, and on a mono device it cancels completely. Anything you widen gets quieter or disappears in mono. Measured on a real track, the mono fold loss went from 0.31 dB at Narrow to 1.12 dB at Extra wide. Always check the Mono fold figure and the "Listen in mono" button after raising width.

16. Vocal enhancer

Works on the centre channel only, so it does not brighten what sits in the stereo sides.

SettingPresence at 3 kHzAir above 11 kHzLeveling
Light+1 dB+0.5 dBup to 1.5 dB
Medium+2 dB+1 dBup to 2.5 dB
Strong+3 dB+1.5 dBup to 3.5 dB

The leveling stage runs first, trimming the 1–4 kHz range when it jumps well above its own average, which evens out a vocal that lunges forward on loud phrases.

Without separated stems the app cannot distinguish a vocal from anything else in the centre, so this also lifts the snare crack, the kick click, and centred guitars. If it turns harsh, drop a level or raise the de-essing. For real vocal-only processing, use Stem balance with per-stem mastering instead.

On an instrumental this control just brightens the centre. It is not useful there.

17. Pumper

Ducks the mid and high bands each time the kick hits, producing the rhythmic pumping common in dance and electronic music. Bass and kick are left at full level, so the low end stays solid.

SettingMaximum duck
Light2 dB
Medium4 dB
Heavy6 dB

It follows the kick itself rather than a tempo grid, using the low band's own level as the trigger, so it needs no BPM setting and does not drift on music with a loose tempo. The attack is 3 ms and the release 150 ms.

This is a creative effect, not a corrective one. Light is audible; Heavy is a production choice. Measured on real material, Medium averaged 0.7 dB of ducking with a 4 dB peak — the average is low because it only acts on kick transients.

19. The A/B player and the mono check

A and B switch instantly between the original and the master while playback continues, so you hear the difference rather than reconstructing it from memory.

Match loudness is on by default and should stay on. Louder reliably sounds better to human ears regardless of quality, so comparing a −10 LUFS master against a −16 LUFS original tells you nothing except which is louder. Turn it off only when you specifically want to hear the loudness gain.

Listen in mono sums both channels live. Use it while a section loops, and listen for elements that thin out, lose their reverb, or disappear — wide synths, double-tracked guitars and stereo reverb are the usual casualties.

The Mono fold figure in the results quantifies the same thing. For reference, a genuinely mono source reads 0.00 dB and a fully out-of-phase one reads above 50 dB. Normal stereo music sits well under 1 dB.

20. The spectrum view

Two modes:

  • Spectrum — the average spectrum of the original in grey and the master in colour, from about 30 Hz to 20 kHz. With references loaded, their averaged curve appears as a dashed line and each individual reference as a fainter line, so you can see both the target and any outlier.
  • Difference — a bar per band showing the change, with the largest boost and cut named underneath.

Match levels is on by default and aligns the two curves so you see tonal change rather than the master simply being louder.

It shows the whole-song average, so it will not reveal how a single section changed. A chorus that distorts will not show up here.

21. What this tool cannot do

  • Fix a mix. If instruments fight for the same frequencies in the centre, no mastering chain resolves it. Clarity and stem balance reduce the symptom; the cure is in the mix.
  • Restore lost quality. Mastering a 128 kbps MP3 cannot recover the high frequencies the encoder discarded.
  • Produce Dolby Atmos. Atmos is object-based and must be built from multitracks in a renderer inside a DAW, then delivered as an ADM BWF file. Upmixing a stereo master is explicitly not accepted by distributors.
  • Replace your ears. Every automatic decision here is a measurement plus an assumption. The measurements are sound; the assumptions are mine.
  • Match a professional mastering engineer. What you get is competent, consistent and free, and it is genuinely useful for demos, references and loudness work. It is not a substitute for someone who has heard ten thousand records on a tuned system.

22. Troubleshooting

Stem separation fails or says it cannot run

  • Opened from a folder (the address starts with file://). Browsers block the background workers separation needs. Use the hosted copy, or run python3 -m http.server in the project folder and open http://localhost:8000.
  • “Runtime could not be loaded.” The app ships its own copy, so this means the files in ort/ are missing and the backup CDN is blocked too. Re-download the project, or check ad blockers and firewalls.
  • “Ran out of memory.” Pick fewer workers, close other tabs, or separate only the 30-second section.
